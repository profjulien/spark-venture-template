# A page that converts

One page, one job: turn a visitor into a yes to your ask (`the-ask.md`). `/new-page` builds to
this and checks against it.

## Their words, first

A visitor decides in seconds whether the page is about *their* problem.
They recognise their own words. So the copy comes from
`evidence/language.md` — the phrases your customers used, sorted with the
problem space canvas — and from nowhere else.

- **Keep the sentence they said.** Filling the canvas tempts you to
  summarise: "motivation for progress" is your analysis. Their sentence is
  the copy. Keep it in the language they spoke, with your translation beside it.
- **Use their names for things:** the problem, the category, the fix they
  use today ("my roster", "the WhatsApp group").
- **Draft the headline as "Now you can…"** in their words, then trim.
  Write five; keep the one a customer would say. When your segment knows
  the problem and keeps putting it off, lead with their struggle instead,
  in their words.
- **Match their register.** A blunt first-person struggle lands in some
  markets and costs face in others. The five-second test tells you which.
- **Ban the platitudes:** better, faster, easy, seamless, all-in-one,
  convenient, revolutionary. Say what, exactly.

Method: Matt Lerner, *Finding Language/Market Fit*, First Round Review.

## Each line carries its label

A line on the page is as strong as the people behind it.

- **Two or more people said it** (a SETTLED canvas box): EVIDENCE. It goes on the page.
- **One person said it** (a PARTIAL box, or n=1): INFERENCE. It may go on
  the page; the experiment is testing it.
- **Nobody said it yet** (an UNTOUCHED box): UNKNOWN. It stays off the
  page, and the canvas heading goes on the interview list.

Each line traces to one quote. The quote stays in `evidence/`; the page
carries the claim. While *The progress they seek* is UNKNOWN, the page
has no headline yet: interview before you build.

## From canvas to page

The canvas is sorted for analysis; the page is sorted for attention. Each
section draws on its own boxes. Example: a made-up rostering tool for clinics.

| Section | Canvas box in `language.md` | Write it so that… | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hero — headline** | The progress they seek, or Push of the situation | their "I was trying to…" becomes "Now you can…", with their verbs | *Publish next week's roster before Friday lunch.* |
| **Hero — line under** | Who struggles | role and circumstance together, narrow enough that the wrong visitor leaves | *For clinic owners in KL who still roster on WhatsApp.* |
| **The problem** | The struggling circumstance, Push of the situation | the trigger moment comes back to them, then their current fix, named fairly, and where it fails | *A nurse calls in sick at 7am and you rebuild the week from your phone. Today: a WhatsApp group and a photo of the whiteboard.* |
| **What you get** | Pull of the new way | three outcomes, each concrete. Before a product exists, Pull is the thinnest box: these lines are INFERENCE, and they are what the experiment tests | |
| **Proof** | your own record | see *Proof before your first customer* | |
| **The deal** | Inertia of the present | the give takes the effort of switching off them | *We set it up with you. Your WhatsApp group stays.* |
| **Questions** | Anxiety of change | one question per worry they voiced, answered plainly | |
| **Link preview** | The struggling circumstance | the `og:title` and `og:description` in the page's `<head>` use the same words as the hero. In a WhatsApp group or a LinkedIn post, the preview is what they see first | |

The hero, the problem and what you get sell. The deal and the questions
lower the risk of saying yes.

## Proof before your first customer

Until the first commitments arrive, prove that you listened:

- **Who you are:** the founders' faces and names, and why you.
- **Who you spoke with:** a real count — *We spoke with 14 clinic owners since August.*
- **Where you stand:** the stage, plainly — *We are onboarding the first
  10 clinics by hand.* Your first visitors come from your own network, so
  an honest page protects it.
- **Interview quotes**, verbatim and with permission, about the problem.

Once people commit, they become the proof: a quote, a first name, a photo
with the product, the count itself.

## The order

1. **Hero** — headline, one line under it, the ask button, one line of
   proof, and a real photo of the product. On a phone, everything but the
   photo shows before any scroll; the photo follows right under.
2. **The problem** — the situation, in the words your customers used.
3. **What you get** — three outcomes, each one concrete.
4. **Proof** — real quotes, real numbers, real faces (with permission).
5. **The deal** — what they give, what they get, what happens next, the
   terms (for a payment: price, refund, delivery window), and an honest
   reason to act now.
6. **Questions** — the three objections you heard most, answered.
7. **Last call** — the button again, and the list for anyone who wants to wait.

## The five-second test

Run it before you publish, and again after every change to the hero.

1. Write down the three or four points the page must carry.
2. Show the page to five people from your segment for five seconds each:
   the phone view and the link preview, as they will see it.
3. Hide it and ask: *What is this? Who is it for? Is that you? What would
   you do next?*
4. It passes when four of the five say back at least three of the points,
   in their own words. Rewrite until it does. Then publish.

Friends may take the test; their answers stay out of your count. The test
checks understanding. The count of asks measures whether they want it.

**Passed, and still few ask clicks?** The visitors understood the page
and the problem is too weak for them to act on. Stop editing the copy:
go back to interviews and the canvas.

## The checklist

- **Every phrase traceable to `evidence/language.md`** — or cut.
- **Every line labelled** EVIDENCE or INFERENCE in the build report; UNKNOWN lines are off the page.
- **Headline = the outcome they want**, in their words. The line under it
  says what it is and who it is for, narrowly.
- **One primary action.** Every button on the page leads to the ask.
  The list is the single fallback.
- **The button says what happens:** *Reserve mine — S$50, refundable* beats
  *Submit*.
- **Proof sits next to the ask.** A quote or a number beside each button.
- **Risk reversal at the button:** refundable, until when, and when it ships.
- **A reason to act now that is true:** a first batch of 30, a founding
  price until a date. Invented scarcity costs you the trust you need.
- **Objections answered in the FAQ**, drawn from what people actually asked.
- **Message match:** a visitor from a post sees that post's words in the
  headline, and in the link preview. One page variant per channel (`/new-page`, `?ref=`).
- **Specific beats clever.** Numbers, names of things, the real price.
- **Fast and light on a phone:** compressed photos, minimal navigation.
- **Five-second test passed** on the phone view and the link preview.
- **Measured:** visitors → engaged → clicked → confirmed, per channel
  (`measure.md`). Change one thing at a time and watch the stage it targets.
